Hiring a web developer is easy to rush. You see a portfolio you like, message a few people, and pick the fastest reply or the lowest number. Months later, the arguments are rarely about colour choices—they are about scope, ownership, communication, and what “done” meant.

Use these questions in discovery calls. You are not grilling people for sport; you are checking fit before money and timelines get tangled.

## Questions about fit and relevant experience

### What projects are closest to ours?

Ask them to walk through one or two builds similar in complexity—not only visual polish. Listen for decisions, constraints, and outcomes. You can also compare against public work samples such as those in [work](/#work), including [YogiSpeaks](/#case-yogispeaks) or [AiVoxa Labs](/#case-aivoxa-labs), to calibrate what “complete” looks like.

### Who will actually do the work?

Agencies and freelancers both delegate at times. Ask who designs, who develops, who writes content (if included), and who you will message day to day. Model differences are covered in [freelance developer vs agency](/blog/freelance-developer-vs-agency).

### Have you worked with our CMS or stack before?

Experience matters more than fashion. If they recommend a new stack, ask why it fits your maintenance reality—not only why it is interesting.

## Questions about scope and process

### How do you turn my notes into a written scope?

A reliable process produces a document with pages/templates, integrations, revision rounds, and exclusions. If everything stays in chat threads, expect drift.

### What do you need from me to start?

Content, brand assets, access credentials, legal pages, and decision-makers’ availability all affect the calendar. Content readiness also affects [professional website cost](/blog/professional-website-cost).

### How are revisions handled?

Clarify rounds, what counts as a revision versus a new feature, and how feedback should be collected (one consolidated list beats twelve conflicting emails).

### What does your typical timeline look like?

Ask for phases: discovery, design, build, QA, launch. Then ask what usually causes delay. Honest answers mention client feedback loops and third-party APIs—not only “it depends.”

## Questions about communication

### How often will we sync?

Weekly updates work for many brochure sites; active builds may need shorter loops. Agree on tools: email, WhatsApp, Notion, Linear, or similar—one primary channel.

### How do you handle blockers?

You want a developer who surfaces risks early: missing copy, unclear sitemap, flaky APIs. Silence until the deadline is a process failure.

## Questions about quality and launch

### How do you QA on mobile and desktop?

Ask which devices/browsers they check and whether forms are tested end to end. Mobile behaviour is commercial, not cosmetic—see [mobile-responsive design for business growth](/blog/mobile-responsive-design-business-growth).

### What SEO foundations are included?

Titles, headings, sitemap, redirects, analytics—confirm what is in scope. Use the [technical SEO checklist](/blog/technical-seo-checklist-modern-websites) as a shared reference so “SEO included” means something concrete.

### What does launch day include?

DNS, SSL, backups, redirects, search console, form notifications. Align with a [website launch checklist](/blog/website-launch-checklist).

> If a candidate cannot explain how launch and handoff work, you may be buying a design file with an unpaid production phase attached.

## Questions about ownership and access

### Who owns the code, design files, and accounts?

You generally want the business to own the domain, hosting, repo, CMS, and design source. Vendor lock-in should be explicit if it exists at all.

### Will we get documentation or training?

Even a short loom video and admin checklist reduces dependency for routine edits.

### Where will the project live during development?

Staging URLs, version control, and backup practices are signs of professional delivery.

## Questions about money and risk

### What is fixed versus estimated?

Fixed-scope builds need firm boundaries. Exploratory product work may need weekly capacity. Mixing the two without saying so creates conflict.

### What is the payment schedule?

Common patterns tie payments to milestones: start, design approval, launch. Avoid 100% upfront for unproven vendors and 100% on completion for long builds without cashflow planning.

### What happens if we pause the project?

Ask about pause fees, file handoff, and how incomplete work is delivered.

## Questions about life after launch

### What support is included after go-live?

Bug-fix windows differ from ongoing maintenance retainers. Read [post-launch website support](/blog/post-launch-website-support) and the [website maintenance guide](/blog/website-maintenance-guide) before you assume free forever support.

### How are change requests priced later?

You want a clear hourly or packaged rate for enhancements.

## How to score answers without a spreadsheet cult

After two or three calls, rate each candidate on:

1. Clarity of scope language
2. Relevance of past work
3. Comfort explaining trade-offs
4. Honesty about risks and dependencies
5. Cultural fit for how your team decides

Ignore theatrical confidence. Prefer people who say “we should decide X before estimating Y.” Prefer written follow-ups that match what was said on the call.

If quotes differ wildly, the scopes probably differ. Ask each vendor to price the same bullet list. Cost interpretation tips live in [professional website cost](/blog/professional-website-cost). Delivery model trade-offs are in [freelance developer vs agency](/blog/freelance-developer-vs-agency).

## Trial tasks and paid discovery

Unpaid speculative redesigns waste everyone’s time and bias toward vendors who can afford free work. Prefer:

- A paid discovery workshop with a written sitemap and risks list
- A small paid prototype of one risky integration
- A code or architecture review of an existing site before a rebuild

Discovery fees that credit toward the build are common and fair when the same partner continues. If you switch vendors after discovery, you still keep the thinking you paid for.

## Reference checks that actually help

Ask previous clients:

- Did communication stay clear mid-project?
- How were delays handled?
- Was handoff complete (access, docs, training)?
- Would you hire them again for maintenance?

Listen for patterns around responsiveness and ownership more than praise for “beautiful design.”

Once hired, protect the relationship with a [website launch checklist](/blog/website-launch-checklist) and an explicit support conversation using [post-launch website support](/blog/post-launch-website-support). Feature priorities for service sites remain a useful shared reference: [essential website features for service businesses](/blog/essential-website-features-service-businesses).

## Warning signs during discovery calls

Walk away—or slow down—if you see:

- Guarantees about rankings or lead volume tied to the build
- Refusal to discuss ownership of accounts and code
- Vague “unlimited pages” language with no template definition
- Pressure to pay everything upfront with no milestones
- Dismissal of mobile testing or form QA as unnecessary

Healthy partners welcome precise questions. They may not have every answer immediately, but they know how they will find out. Technical foundations you can reference together include the [technical SEO checklist](/blog/technical-seo-checklist-modern-websites) and speed guidance such as the [WordPress speed optimization checklist](/blog/wordpress-speed-optimization-checklist).

## Closing: hire for process, not only portfolio polish

The best hiring conversations feel practical. You leave with a clearer scope, known risks, and a sense of how the person communicates under ambiguity.
