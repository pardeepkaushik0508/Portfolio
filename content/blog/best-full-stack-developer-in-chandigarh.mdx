## What “full stack” should mean for your business

Full stack is a broad label. In practice, you want someone who can own the path from brief to production: information architecture, UI implementation, backend logic, data storage, integrations and deployment. For many Chandigarh businesses that also means comfort with WordPress, WooCommerce or Shopify when those tools are the right fit—and the judgment to recommend a custom stack when they are not.

A useful full stack developer explains *why* a stack fits your goals. They will discuss content editing needs, SEO constraints, payment flows, admin workflows and how the site will be maintained after launch. Vague promises of “everything modern” without tying decisions to your operations are a warning sign.

> Treat “full stack” as end-to-end ownership of outcomes, not as a buzzword checklist. Ask what they will personally ship versus what they will outsource.

## Skills that matter more than buzzwords

When you evaluate candidates, map skills to your project type.

**For marketing and lead-generation sites**, look for semantic HTML, accessible components, solid Core Web Vitals habits, clean CMS setup and measurable conversion paths. [Next.js SEO fundamentals](/blog/nextjs-seo-guide) become especially relevant if you choose a React-based stack.

**For eCommerce**, confirm experience with product data, checkout friction, inventory sync and payment gateways. Compare platform paths using [Shopify vs WooCommerce](/blog/shopify-vs-woocommerce) before you hire for a store build.

**For custom apps**, dig into authentication, role-based access, API design, database modeling, error handling and staging environments. Ask how they test critical flows and how they roll back a bad deploy.

Soft skills are part of the technical hire. You want written updates you can understand, honest scope conversations and documented decisions. A developer who only demos code in isolation—without discussing business constraints—often creates expensive rework.

## A practical evaluation framework

Use the same scorecard for every shortlisted developer:

1. **Brief understanding** — Can they restate your goals, users and constraints in their own words?
2. **Proposed approach** — Do they outline phases, assumptions and open questions?
3. **Relevant proof** — Do live projects resemble your complexity, not only your industry logo?
4. **Communication cadence** — Weekly updates, shared tools, decision logs?
5. **Post-launch plan** — Backups, monitoring, content training, support windows?
6. **Commercial clarity** — Fixed milestones, hourly boundaries, change-request rules?

Run a paid discovery sprint when the project is large. A short paid workshop (requirements, sitemap, technical risks) reveals more than an unpaid “quote battle.” It also protects both sides from mismatched expectations.

## Red flags to catch early

Watch for these patterns during discovery calls:

- Guarantees of first-page rankings or “best in city” claims without process
- No staging environment or no backup strategy
- Refusing to put scope and assumptions in writing
- Copy-paste proposals that ignore your brief
- Unwillingness to discuss maintenance or knowledge transfer
- Stack recommendations that only match what they already sell, never what you need

Also be cautious of extremely low fixed prices for complex work. Underpriced projects often get rushed, stalled or handed off mid-stream. Price should reflect risk, research and accountability—not just screen hours.

## Cost, timeline and scope: how to compare fairly

Ask every candidate to price the *same* written brief. Include must-have pages, integrations, content source, languages, admin roles and launch constraints. Then compare:

- What is included versus optional
- Number of revision rounds
- Who provides copy, photos and product data
- Hosting and domain responsibilities
- Training and warranty period

A cheaper quote that excludes SEO setup, analytics, redirects or content migration is not cheaper—it is incomplete. For planning ranges and line items, keep [professional website cost](/blog/professional-website-cost) open while you review proposals.

Timelines should include content freeze dates. Many “developer delays” are actually waiting on approvals, assets or legal copy. A strong partner will call that out upfront and build buffers into milestones.

## Interview questions that reveal real capability

Skip trivia quizzes. Ask scenario questions:

- “Our current site ranks for key terms. How would you migrate without losing traffic?”
- “We need editors who are not technical. How will they update content safely?”
- “Checkout failed for some users last month. How would you diagnose that?”
- “What would you refuse to build in v1, and why?”

Listen for structured thinking. Good answers mention trade-offs, measurement and rollback. Weak answers jump straight to tools without clarifying the problem.

You can also request a short loom-style walkthrough of a past project: architecture, hardest bug, and what they would improve today. That single artifact often separates polished marketers from builders who own outcomes.

## After you hire: set the project up to succeed

Once you choose a partner, protect the relationship with clear operating rules:

- Single source of truth for requirements (shared doc or ticket board)
- Named decision-maker on your side
- Staging URL for every meaningful release
- Definition of done for each milestone
- Launch checklist covering redirects, forms, analytics and backups

Plan a soft launch for high-risk flows. Collect feedback from a small internal group before public announcement. Schedule a post-launch review two weeks later to catch usability and performance issues while context is fresh.
